### Growth Pill 1. A dirty little secret

The year I studied in the U.S., not even three years after my first design class, I was asked to produce 50 logos in a 90-minute class. Back home Graphic Design was part of a Comms degree. Practical exercises would happen in the computer lab, still in [Freehand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adobe_FreeHand). Forms would be the result of adding and subtracting basic shapes and typing colors in an input box. Overseas it was within an arts degree, and the request found me in a workshop, in front of a big table with lots of paint drippings on it, a large piece of paper, and one of the fat Sharpies in my right hand. A week wouldn’t have been enough to do a single logo, now I was supposed to produce 50 in an hour?

It was one of the best exercises I’ve ever done. Try it yourself:

1.  Using a big marker draw on a long piece of paper as many symbols as you can. The aim is to do it quickly and without much detail. Get your hand loose.
    
2.  Look at what you did. There were 8 of us in class, so there were 400 marks to look at after barely 10 minutes.
    
3.  Pick your favorites, two or three will stand out.
    
4.  Now on a new piece of paper do 50 copies of each. Again, fast hand. Switch your rational brain off.
    
5.  Pick again. One will have the right balance. Will feel just right. Scan it, vectorize it. There you go.
    

If, like me, you’ve spent a good part of your career doing clean lines, straight angles, and grid-based work, there are few things as refreshing as realizing that you can just put a bunch of marks, or blobs, or stains on a piece of paper—try watercolors too and let me know how it goes—and bring the prettiest over to the computer.

You’ll think you’re training your hand—you are—but actually you are learning to look at the world with fresh eyes.

### Rambling 1. Abstract

If you are in Málaga this summer don’t leave without a quick visit to [Centre Pompidou](https://centrepompidou-malaga.eu/) and its temporary exhibition **[Gesture and matter](https://centrepompidou-malaga.eu/expo/el-gesto-y-la-materia/)**, even if it is only for the chance of unexpectedly standing in front of a [Pollock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackson_Pollock) for a few minutes.

> After World War II, a new artistic movement – abstract, gestural and matterist – spread out from Paris. With the trauma of war and techniques of mass extermination laid bare, artists felt that they needed to wipe the slate clean, to “start from scratch as if painting didn’t exist” ([Barnett Newman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnett_Newman)).

Abstract expressionists did this so well that they became the image—and for some, the caricature—of what contemporary art is. One that has already lasted 70 years, and counting.

I always come back to this movement because of the way in which it dialogues with its figurative past. The masters painting a scene as if it were a photograph always had reality itself to check whether they had done a good job or not. Never easy, but they explored that forest with a map in their hands. Their abstract heirs went on a similar search but without knowing where they’d end up. Trusting their pulse and conviction, knowing that a single wrong turn, a stain out of place may ruin the whole work.

The surprise when I walked into that room is that they can still look at us straight in the eye. In the middle of our obsession with image-making, in the time of social media and [Midjourney](https://www.midjourney.com/), each one of these paintings transcends the mere image that imprints on your eyes. They are material, an instant frozen in time, a calligraphic dance captured on canvas. Nobody could have theorized them. They had to be painted first.

I don’t care what you read, no image model—nor your five-year-old nephew—will ever be capable of that.

### Growth Pill 2. Where to look

The true unlock from the exercise above comes when you realize you are not looking at the painting or the logo in front of you; you are looking at yourself. What are you fixating on? Is it a detail or the whole image? Is it something you are loving or something that feels off? Is it the color choice? The shape? The texture? What can you learn from that? Or, if it is your own work, how can you fix it?

In a museum full of paintings, in front of your moodboard, or reviewing your explorations, let your eyes lead you first so the brain can ask questions later and not the other way around. And don’t forget about that [little voice we’ve talked about before](https://designsobremesa.substack.com/p/012-the-sound-of-our-own-voice), that will show the way.

Hand-eye coordination remains a core skill in art, design, and creativity. Practice now, and thank me when you realize it applies to vibe coding too.
