### Rambling 1. Build-first design

Growing up in a city like Salamanca, where the old kingdoms of Castille and Leon once met, we’ve heard a million times the difference between the romanesque and the gothic styles. It is a fascinating case study that takes us all the way to ancient Greece.

Greeks would place a slab of horizontal rock directly sitting on two columns. This limited what could be built. Stone works better under compression than under tension, so too much weight over the slab, would cause the bottom of the rock to bend, creating tension under which it would eventually give. Someone in that Roman empire that was absorbing the richness of ancient Greece must have figured this out: by carefully cutting the pieces of stone to form a semi-circle, all the weight of the arch would be distributed through compression directly to the columns. The solution was so good, it wouldn’t need any mortar to keep the rocks together. (The aqueduct in Segovia is a great example of a huge structure built this way).

This construction method carried on after the empire’s collapse in a style that was, aptly named, romanesque. And it would also have its limits, requiring thicker and thicker walls the larger and larger you wanted to build. If you want to recognise a romanesque church, look for semi-circle arches, sturdy look, and thick walls.

We credit the next innovation to the goths. Instead of drawing a semicircle of the exact distance, they would trace a larger one (sometimes twice the size). Starting from each side, the two halves of the arch would meet in a pointy shape. Again, distribution of weight was better, walls could be thinner, grow beautiful windows and make the whole structure taller until you end up with [Notre Dame in Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notre-Dame_de_Paris), or [Burgos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burgos_Cathedral) or [León](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le%C3%B3n_Cathedral) in Spain.

### Rambling 2. The no-tax

Another week, another CEO saying stuff. This time, playing very close to the line of rage-bait, Grant Lee, CEO of Gamma (an AI for slide decks) calls the time spent formatting slides (instead of the serious business of “strategizing”) a **design tax**.

Look, I’m not even going to bother counter arguing this. One, twitter is now full of responses; two, we all here know what each one of us would say to this on how design adds value and has to be seen as an investment and not a cost and yada yada yada. Not worth it.

What is worth reflecting on is that this dynamic, although with new tools, is not new. Many of us happily jumped on the bandwagon of the no-code but didn’t spend much time on the no-design solutions that were already crowding the space. Or at least, we didn’t give it a name although many would have pointed at [Canva](http://canva.com/es_es/) as a cause for concern in pre-AI design. Actually, many no-code had a good amount of no-design too – built with editors that allowed non-coders to push slick websites live, while at the same time coming packed with great templates that would do the trick for most businesses (I’m looking at you, [WordPress](https://wordpress.org/), [Webflow](https://webflow.com/), [Squarespace](https://www.squarespace.com/)).

We all have a natural drive to keep control of our own creation. We’ve wanted to figure out coding because we were underwhelmed with the way our designs were finally implemented. It is only normal that Engineers or Strategists find ways to keep control of the way their ideas look. Vibe-coding is both no-code and no-design. While it is scary to look at from the point of view in which a non-expert could cut us out of the equation, it creates a new “soup” in which we all can cross-pollinate through each other’s areas of expertise.

I don’t think the future is going to be as simple as a bunch of specific roles getting automated. To me it is looking more like the tools will enable us to broaden our profiles, and gain expertise beyond the borders of our discipline. Great design will still be needed and searched for, but maybe we just have to go back to building it first, like our medieval architects.

### Share. Salamanca by proxy

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!axCz!,w_2400,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8264fa91-22dd-4241-bb5e-9e4e21a122a2_4916x2048.png)

The main reason for Salamanca to be on any map is hosting the fourth oldest University in Europe, founded in 1218 after Bologna, Oxford, and Cambridge. It was an academic powerhouse of the Middle Ages. This year, for example, they are celebrating the 500th anniversary of the _[school of Salamanca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_of_Salamanca)_, a remarkable generation of scholars that were pioneers in the foundations of economy and natural law. In the dawn of the conquest of America, some of them argued and fought against the big powers of the time (the King and the Pope) for a universal perspective on human rights (which traces its lineage all the way to the UN’s declaration, over four centuries later). Its reputation grew so big that the city (by proxy of the university) got its own saying:

> _Quod natura non dat, Salmantica non praestat_  
> (What nature doesn’t give, Salamanca won’t lend)

Not even having the biggest memory and the largest knowledge can give you the brains to know what to do with it. While this definitely rhymes with my take on AI, and how it needs us on the driving seat, there is something more powerful that got me thinking about this.

Here, you are reading someone that got from Salamanca (and [its great professors](https://designsobremesa.substack.com/p/designing-from-the-plateau)) a passionate introduction to design, a craft that enables us to create whatever nature didn’t give us.
