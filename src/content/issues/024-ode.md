### Pause 3. To vacations

The first time taking Charlotte to Spain was both about family and holidays. A week in a seaside resort in [Matalascañas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matalasca%C3%B1as) where we’d been taking my grandparents as our favourite family tradition. It was the year in which we fell in love, travelled together for the first time, and then retreated to our own flats to make it through three months of lockdown. It felt like throwing her, Canadian, successful, and sophisticated, into the deep end of Spain in one move. I was mildly terrified.

> It won’t be fancy or luxurious, but it will be as honest as it gets

While Donald Trump came to power promising to [drain the swamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drain_the_swamp) of crooked politicians, in Spain the socials started talking about a _charca_ filled with an affluent middle class that would choose convenience over a shiny experience. As if their own pick—exclusive in appearance but as crowded as travel is these days—were a bucolic garden that would impress their non-existing followers. You shall not choose a chain Italian restaurant for a Valentine’s date and you shall not enjoy your holidays in a tacky hotel. You shall do more with them than jumping between the pool and the buffet.

Holidays in a seaside resort were, first, something my parents chose for me when I was a kid. Then my grandparents thought it was a good idea for themselves. As they got older, the whole family rallied together to make sure they would enjoy those two weeks under the sun. Now it is purely ours. Endless fun for a three-year-old and her grandma. Swimming in the pool until we look like prunes. Walks in the sunset hunting for some _[pescaito frito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pescado_frito)_. And a poolside [Maxibon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxibon) as soon as everyone wakes up from their siestas.

Sorry, you won’t see this on my Instagram—I wouldn’t want the crowds to find out.
