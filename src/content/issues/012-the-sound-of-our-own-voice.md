### Confession. A noisy place

I have to confess. This week I’ve spent too much time thinking about a tweet. This one in particular

Read again. Steve Jobs, Dieter Rams, James Dyson. We waste hours and hours pontificating about design and it is always the same handful of names. Add Olivetti, Braun and Sony and you have your own volume on History of Design. But this is a rant for another day. Let’s click on something else first.

If you haven’t spent enough time around designers I’ll let you in on a secret: we insist on the [“form follows function”](https://designsobremesa.substack.com/p/008-i-think-i-like-the-terminal-now) so we never forget the first commandment of good design:

> You shall not care about aesthetics alone.

Aesthetics are vapid, pointless and subject of artists. We are solving hard problems. We design so it can be used, and that use should drive the shapes that we put on the screen and nothing else. Any doubts? Check the works of the people above; they are not that many. Let’s click again.

The other confession is that Dyson wasn’t on my radar until I moved to the UK. Here he is admired and beloved. With him you stumble into an aura that at moments matches the fascination around Dieter Rams or Jonny Ive. A Dyson hairdryer, a fan, or a vacuum cleaner are in the must-have lists of every good designer. They will start by telling you about the unique looks of all these objects but then they’ll insist that it is so much more than that. That those looks are not capricious but respond to some serious engineering to get the best vacuuming experience possible. Industrial designer and **inventor** says Dyson’s bio. Ok, one last click.

So if Dyson’s design is so functional, which hoover is the guy cleaning your office building using a Dyson… or a Henry?

### Growth Pill. A quiet place

All that noise, and somehow we still expect to hear ourselves. To say that a designer should defend their work with confidence is halfway between a platitude and a temerity. I want the designers in my team to always start from a position of curiosity and openness. We don’t have all the answers, plus a lot of joy in our job comes from figuring things out. Go ahead, ask questions, share early and often, gather feedback, adjust, repeat. What can I take out? What can I simplify? What can I try? Are you guys happy with this? Ok, I’ll say it. Do you _like_ it?

Design is a conversation that never ends until it does. Everything clicks. In your brain or on the screen. You see it, and chase it, and draw it as well as you can. And somewhere inside, your inner voice hugs you in the stomach and says: “you kicked ass today.”

And then we go to get feedback again. A critique with peers, a presentation to stakeholders. A thumbs up or a thumbs down.

Things are going to go both ways. You are going to get praise that you feel is unjustified because what you are showing is not as good as it could have been. You worked hard, it is not that. You poured your time and soul on the task and the best thing you could find doesn’t make you happy; yet these people love it. Also, you are going to be torn apart. I’ve had people bursting out laughing at my work when shown in the screen. Things are going to get deprioritised. People won’t agree with the approach. Doubts will creep even though you feel you just shared a slam dunk. Your work will always sound different to others than it sounds to you.

Listen to that little voice. Grow it, prune it, educate it. That’s how self-confidence works. Humble in victory, good sport in defeat, whether they are deserved or not, but always yourself.

Happy Easter, everyone.
