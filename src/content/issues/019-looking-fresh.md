### Aperitivo. Ephemeral Frankfurt

Aperitivo is more than an excuse to drop yet another casual-food-related Spanishism—I know, I know, it can also be an Italianism, I hear you. Frankfurt is [World Design Capital](https://wdc2026.org/en) in 2026 and Aperitivo is the space [READ](https://designread.es/en/home-2/) and [FDCV](https://fundaciodisseny.com/) created for a showcase of Spanish Design.

Instead of a regular conference with a slide deck, a conversation where you could touch the things you were talking about. Since those of us who missed it no longer have the chance to enjoy it, let’s at least get inspired by [Jorge Penadés](https://penades.xyz/)‘ work. He designed and built—or built and designed—a space that, like those conversations, was meant to be ephemeral, lasting only a couple of days:

> “No glue, no staples. Just paper treated like a structural element that, once the event is over, is donated back to the World Design Capital”

Jorge’s work makes me want to drop everything that I’m doing to start building with bent paper. That would already be a great discovery, but that’s already been done.

A staple-less stapler (which, BTW, would deserve its own sobremesa); paper as a construction material; being a designer isn’t enough because, how do you learn to imagine what can’t be thought of?

### Rambling 1. Serendipity

Meeting [Chele Esteve](https://www.linkedin.com/in/chele-esteve-sendra-38b0444a/) was a possibility. She came to [Munich](https://designsobremesa.substack.com/p/018-the-thoughtful-streets-in-europe) from [Universitat Politecnica de Valencia](https://www.upv.es/en) to discuss her research on Metaverse’s accessibility. Grabbing a beer and some Indian dinner with the Spanish gang after a long day of conferences and workshops was a necessity. Finding out about [Chele’s special and personal appreciation of William Morris and his work](https://www.instagram.com/p/DYchpCpIZ_G/) should be just one of the many random topics that we got to jump to and from in those five hours in the afternoon. Next Wednesday, back in London. I pick my daughter up from nursery; they’ve had a bunch of 3 year olds working on their own version of Morris’ patterns. Proper serendipity.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Zog6!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F030b8958-6bd4-4e1b-9a03-6d34c0f5dbfb.heic)

Serendipity, randomness, or chance, I hope this is your reminder to add [William Morris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Morris) to your references if he isn’t already (and [his gallery](https://www.wmgallery.org.uk/) to your To-Visit list). The great thing about the classics is that they remind us that our hopes, aspirations, and headaches are rarely ours. I’ve been for 19 issues already wondering about design, art, craft, engineering. How they all come together? Morris gets off his DeLorean and joins the sobremesa straight from the Victorian era.

### Rambling 2. Subconscious moodboarding

Only someone like Ken Sakatacould come to poke holes at such an established practice like the moodboard. He’s done moodboards, you’ve done them, I’ve done them. Even non designers are almost doing them when they go on pinterest for inspiration for their new kitchen or doing up their bedroom.

“This exercise is too conscious”—Sakata says—”And technology makes it worse.” We consciously decide what goes in our moodboard, we already know where we want to go. We all pick stuff from algorithms that are serving the same stuff. And when we replace Google with Claude we are just doubling down on the issue. How could we break from everyone consuming and creating basically the same stuff? How could we let our subconscious creativity free and take us to unexplored spaces?

He gives himself a limit, 36 photos every week, the film camera is his tool but not a requirement. The aim is not great photos but visual note-taking. At the end of the week those photos go in the board first, and then comes the conscious exercise of understanding why they are there, what the connections might be. I’m already looking at how could I bring this process into my day to day.

Ken Sakata shouldn’t have ever been in this newsletter in the first place. He was a doctor in Melbourne, started a satirical account during the pandemic that grew to a big enough size to pivot it into a serious fashion business ([If you have time, check out his history in his own words](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tbmN82bYvdE)).

No matter what you think about his clothes, even if fashion design is not that interesting for you, on the way here he’s also mastered the art of the short-form video essay. In very few other places you are going to find such a series of deep and rich reflections on design and creativity. Take the video above as just the first example.

Go ahead, do a bit of doomscrolling because his account is totally worth it. Make sure you stay there until you feel the drive to go and make something. Even if you have no clue what you’re doing.
