### Share 1. Buchstabenwurst

Earlier in the year, [when I visited Valencia, I introduced you to the not-so-user-friendly world of Design Associations](https://designsobremesa.substack.com/p/009-drain-the-slop). In Spain we have regional ones like [ADCV](https://www.adcv.com/en/) (for Valencian Autonomous Region) or [DIME](https://www.dimeasociacion.org/) (For Castille and Leon and the one I’m a member of). All these regionals come together under a bigger umbrella association named [READ](https://designread.es/en/home-2/) (Spanish Network of Design Associations). This structure has its European equivalent in [BEDA, the Bureau for European Design Associations](https://beda.org/).

These lines have been written from Munich over the weekend, where I attended [BEDA’s annual Design Forum](https://beda.org/news/join-beda-in-munich-may-2026-beda-design-forum-and-general-assembly/). A couple of days to meet with a plethora of design voices, each one with their own European accent. A great reminder for someone like me that there is a dense design scene not only working in the private sector but also in the public one and academia.

BEDA is the fourth layer that complements any of the three above: activism and lobbying. One in which we come together not just as designers but as political actors with interests, goals, and the desire to make lives better for our peers and the society we live in.

A great opportunity to shift gears from the uber-value-driven dynamics of designing in a private enterprise.

### Share 2. Sensefinding

“Beyond thoughtless design” was the theme for this year’s Forum, with accessibility taking the stage in the morning. My highlight was the presentation by Latvian designer, educator, and [G42 member](https://madres.beda.org/en/glorious-42/rihards-funts/), [Rihards Funts](https://www.rihardsfunts.eu/post/612939928557551616/i-am-a-designer-curator-educator-and-founder-of), who lost his eyesight a few years ago. Makes most of the themes normally discussed in this newsletter feel like child’s play.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!0Kly!,w_2400,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Faae5271c-58be-4cd8-8221-b684a0d33091_3296x1728.png)

Give me a hammer and everything will look like a nail; give me 15 years designing in digital, and visual accessibility will look like screen readers, color contrast and alt texts. Funts shines a light on something so obvious we rarely think about it.

When I land in a new city, I pull up my phone with Google Maps to know where I’m going, but after the algorithm, the instructions, the turns and the text there is the city. “Make a right after the church”, “leave the supermarket on your left”, “I’ll meet you under the town hall’s clock”. Landmarks can’t be touched, or read, or felt. If you can’t see them, how will your other senses help you find your way?

The sound of traffic can make a screen reader more difficult to hear but also tell you that you made it to the intersection. The texture on the pavement, that indicates directions or that you are too close to the road. Even smell becomes a landmark—now that Charlotte is pregnant again, she will change where she walks to the tube to avoid the smell of the fishmonger that is now too much.

It is usually the more subtle and gentle pointing the finger and asking “have you thought about this?” that resonates the loudest.

### Share 3. Pointmaking

In the afternoon, a conversation on Design Policy moderated by Piotr Swiatek. 15 people sitting at the table from no less than 10 countries. Every single one of them would bring their own flavour to both “Design” and “Europe.” We kicked off with the conclusions of [Design Policy Mapping in Europe report, that BEDA](https://madres.beda.org/en/publications/design-policy-mapping-in-europe/) published last November which paints a mixed picture:

> “On the one hand, explicit design policies are rare. On the other hand, design is more visible and influential than ever.”

This is not enough. BEDA’s ambitions had already been set up by outgoing president Christina Melander.

> “Design must no longer be treated as an afterthought, but embedded as a core driver of decision‑making at political and organisational levels.”

In politics and governance, design wants a seat at the table too.

My friend Kike Corecher, who’s been doing this for way more years than I have, always insists: “it is the right people that will unlock things.” The trust of the right someone that will open the next handful of doors.

In this we are in the same boat as the rest of society. Whether it is through a party, or a union, or a charity, everyone that wants to cause political change is faced with the question of how to make our voices heard.

My hope is that we show up with a gun to this knife fight. Any other group (professional or political) that wants to get into activism is going to have to figure out, almost from the get-go, challenges that should be second nature to us: from branding to storytelling, there is a lot of our own expertise that we can leverage to make our point in the most effective way.

### Share 4. Streetwalking

“European Union Design needs more…” This was the final question to a little interview I recorded during the forum. It came down to a Spanishism: European Union design needs more _“street”_.

In Spanish we say “Te falta calle” (”You lack street”) to say that someone’s knowledge or skills are too detached from everyday life. Now I’m sad I wasn’t quick enough thinking on my feet to honor the t-shirts that Castille and Leon studio and fellow DIME members, [Microbio](https://www.microbio.tv/), printed to raise awareness on the issue of rural depopulation: “No te falta calle, te falta campo” (”You aren’t lacking street, you are lacking countryside”)

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ut61!,w_2400,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F76e485fc-73ad-440b-8caa-0f5c9d793301_739x481.png)

One of our biggest sins is ideating and theorising from designers talking to designers who listen to designers so we can impress other designers. In that loop we love to say the right thing: how important it is to listen to the users, the people, or the citizens; but would those “civilians” relate to any of the things that we discuss when we get together?

Call it street, call it countryside, or simply the rest of the world, we can always remind each other one more time that the best design will start and end in the people that will enjoy it. It is not about convincing them of anything but about listening, paying attention and getting as much out of their way as possible.
