### Memory. The clockwork orange

My grandfather, in one of his multiple lives, owned a fruit shop—This is not [the philosophy teacher that has already dropped by](https://designsobremesa.substack.com/p/013-whos-in-the-room), but the other one. I think it is because of that that he held some pride in walking me, as a kid, to the market that would run every Saturday in Leon’s plaza mayor. It was a three movement dance.

First, at the market, the fruit whisperer. He would touch, tap, smell, and sometimes even listen to them. An exercise in taste before we could actually taste them. Decades of knowledge that words couldn’t explain.

Second, the pride. At lunch or dinner, after all the mains are gone, he would bring the fruit platter to the center of the table and tell me which one to choose. “No, not that one, try this one.” The best orange I’ve ever had. Every single time. And the smug look on his face. A raised eyebrow saying “I told you so.”

Finally, the juice. My line of Alvarez is one of early birds. More times than not, my grandfather, my father, and me, the only ones awake in the flat for an early breakfast: “Do you want some juice?”

“Wait a second, we have to plug it first”—as I was trying to press the first half orange against the juicer—”Try now. No, don’t worry, no switch, no nothing, just press.” It took me years. I’m not even sure he knew. But it was in my first design class when I realised that this fruit wizard from a Roman town near the north of Spain was the first person in my life that admired [Dieter Rams](https://designmuseum.org/discover-design/all-stories/what-is-good-design-a-quick-look-at-dieter-rams-ten-principles) and Braun.

### Confession. An uncomfortable truth

In the 2000s Apple’s fanbase was keen to justify why they chose that funny looking laptop that was compatible with nothing else. For engineers it was UNIX, the efficiency, the teraflops, the closed ecosystem. For everyone else Steve Jobs gave us “DESIGN.” And when we looked at their head of design, [Jony Ive lifted the finger and pointed to the moon](https://designsobremesa.substack.com/i/187752409/confession-its-not-you-its-me).

There we found round glasses, clean lines, the ultimate German-looking minimalism, the bauhaus in all its modernist glory, and even ten commandments principles of great design. A design so inevitable that it makes us believe that we are more engineers than artists.

I danced to that music like crazy. Design was my new religion and I was going to be its most zealous protector.

Apple also closed the loop very nicely for us. Design taking the central stage aligned in time with a big turnaround in their results. “See? Design is key for commercial success”—we would love to say. And if the ultimate source of it were all those amazing Braun products we should follow them, right?

One would think that. Yet, when you google about Braun’s legacy in design, you will find a million pages listing all their greatest hits, but its biggest blockbuster.

### Rambling. Brave new world

It was the little BRAUN on the front that zapped me all the way back to that first design class like in the critic’s scene in Ratatouille. Only that for me, in this trip to the past and back I wasn’t chasing a memory, but the lack of it. In all these years caring about design, repeating Dieter Rams here, and Braun there, nobody seemed to mention this product that apparently is everywhere. When I went to a Rugby World Cup with a handful of team-mates, there would be three of them; when I went snowboarding with another group, four more; when Charlotte and I started dating, she would show up at my place just with the brush in her bag because we had the same one.

Although Oral-B was its own brand that ended, same as Braun, owned by Gillette, the toothbrush would be presented as “Designed by Braun”. The same design studio in Germany, which would still proudly quote the legacy left by the titans in their past. They were sought out not only for their industrial design craft, but for the expert engineering required to figure out its moving parts.

The object itself, of course, looks nothing like what we keep calling ‘Braun’.

In the first design-related narrative on this toothbrush that I’ve come across in all these years, Javier Cañada[resurfaced](https://www.terremoto.net/2014/un-paquete-llega-a-cincinatti) a couple of weeks ago a short story that puts Dieter Rams and the Oral-B face to face. The tone wasn’t celebratory. On the contrary, it would capture a somber tone of sunset. He imagines Dieter Rams quietly watching the presentation of the new toothbrush packaging. Looked like straight out of a bottle of shampoo. The agency even threaded into their presentation praise for the old master. The pivotal moment when the age that will fuel our nostalgia forever is finally left behind for the brave new world to take the stage.

So what do we do with this toothbrush? What do we do with its design? Is its commercial success a sign that it is not worthy of our attention, or should we restore it to the museum of forgotten design next to [Henry](https://designsobremesa.substack.com/p/012-the-sound-of-our-own-voice) and the [monobloc](https://designsobremesa.substack.com/p/006-a-tale-of-two-chairs)?
